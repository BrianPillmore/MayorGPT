# Fiscal Productivity Stack

## Best for
Evaluating whether a major development proposal, annexation, incentive deal, or infrastructure project creates or destroys city financial value.

## When to activate
Pull this stack before council approval of any major development proposal, TIF or incentive request, annexation petition, or new infrastructure project. Use it whenever a headline investment or growth opportunity needs a structured fiscal reality check — not just "does this generate tax revenue?" but "does this generate more tax revenue than it costs to serve, over the full life of the infrastructure?"

## Combine
- Agent: `agents/executive-office/city-manager.md`
- Agent: `agents/statutory-roles/finance-director-budget-officer.md`
- Agent: `agents/departments/planning-and-development-director.md`
- Skills: `fiscal-impact-analysis`, `infrastructure-liability-assessment`, `cip-prioritizer`, `debt-financing-brief`

## Sequence
1. **Planning Director agent + fiscal-impact-analysis** — open every major proposal with a full fiscal impact analysis before any staff recommendation is written. Attach the application or project description, site plan, current assessor data for the parcel, and the city's most recent cost-of-services data. The output is a fiscal impact summary table showing annual revenue, annual cost of services, net annual contribution, capital investment required, and payback period.
2. **Finance Director agent + infrastructure-liability-assessment** — before adding new infrastructure obligations, establish what the city already owes on its existing infrastructure. Attach the current CIP, the most recent asset management study or facility condition assessment, and the current maintenance budget by asset category. The output is the city's current infrastructure liability gap — the annual hidden deficit between what infrastructure requires and what is being spent. This context is essential: a city that is already underfunding maintenance by $2 million per year should evaluate new infrastructure obligations against that baseline, not in isolation.
3. **Finance Director agent + debt-financing-brief** — if the proposal requires public capital investment or debt issuance, run the financing brief with the fiscal solvency check applied. Attach the fiscal impact analysis from step 1 and the infrastructure liability assessment from step 2 so the brief can assess whether the city's infrastructure obligation ratio supports taking on new debt. Flag any issuance that finances infrastructure for a development that did not show positive net fiscal return in step 1.
4. **City Manager agent + cip-prioritizer** — if the proposal triggers additions to the CIP, score the new items against the existing CIP list using the maintenance-first principle. New capacity projects for a development that has not demonstrated positive net fiscal return should score below deferred maintenance on existing assets. Attach the current CIP list and condition assessments.
5. **City Manager agent** — synthesize the outputs from steps 1–4 into a decision memo for council. The memo should state plainly: total public cost (capital investment plus ongoing service cost); projected net fiscal return and the year it turns positive (if ever); how the new infrastructure obligation compares to the city's existing infrastructure liability gap; and what the recommended action is, with the fiscal rationale stated explicitly.

## Watch for
- **Headline revenue without service cost offset**: The most common mistake in development analysis is comparing a project's projected tax revenue to zero rather than to the cost of services the project will generate. A $500,000 annual tax contribution from a development that costs $450,000 per year to serve is a $50,000 annual net gain — not a half-million-dollar win. Always require the full cost-of-services calculation before interpreting any revenue figure.
- **Infrastructure obligation outlasting the deal**: Incentive agreements, TIF districts, and annexation agreements often create public infrastructure obligations that are permanent while the financial benefits are time-limited or conditional. Roads and utilities built today will need replacement in 30–50 years regardless of what the abatement agreement says. Flag any deal where infrastructure is built before the incentive period ends, because the city will begin paying maintenance costs before it receives full tax revenue.
- **Deferred maintenance as hidden context**: A city that is already falling behind on infrastructure maintenance does not have a clean balance sheet. New growth that is marginally cash-flow positive may still make the city's overall financial position worse if it adds infrastructure faster than the tax base grows. The infrastructure-liability-assessment step is not optional — it provides the context that determines whether marginal new revenue is actually helpful.
- **Optimistic assessed value projections**: Fiscal impact analyses frequently use developer-supplied assessed value projections that assume full buildout, top-of-market values, and no vacancies. Use comparable assessed values from the city's own tax roll, not projections, and run a downside scenario at 75 percent of the projected value. The fiscal case for a project should hold at the downside, not just at optimistic assumptions.
- **Per-unit cost of services underestimation**: Cost-of-services estimates are frequently understated because they exclude indirect costs (administrative overhead, general government), ignore the geographic premium for serving dispersed development (longer road and utility runs, longer emergency response times), and fail to include the capital component of service delivery (fleet replacement, facility maintenance). Require that the fiscal-impact-analysis step use fully loaded costs, not just direct departmental expenditures.
