# Small City Starter Guide: Using AI Without a Dedicated IT Department

This guide is for cities that do not have a dedicated IT department, a formal AI policy, or an AI specialist on staff — which describes the overwhelming majority of American cities.

If you are a mayor or council member with good instincts but no tech background, a city clerk who also handles records, communications, and agenda preparation, or a city manager trying to get more done with a lean team, this is where to start.

You do not need to understand how AI works to use it safely. You need three things: a clear decision about what tool is approved, a short list of what staff cannot put into it, and a rule that a human always reviews the output before it becomes official.

---

## Who this is for

Most AI guidance is written for large cities with technology directors, legal teams, and dedicated policy staff. This document is not that.

This is for the city of 3,000 where the clerk runs the agenda, the public records log, and the social media account. It is for the small town where the mayor answers constituent emails on nights and weekends. It is for the mid-size city that has been running lean since the last recession and has not been able to hire back the positions it lost.

According to ICMA's 2024 survey, only 10 percent of local governments have staff tasked with overseeing AI, and only 9 percent have a formal org-wide AI policy. This means most American cities are making it up as they go — often with individual employees using personal AI accounts for government work. That creates real legal exposure and real risk of a mistake reaching the public.

You do not need to be a technology city to use AI responsibly. You need a starting point.

---

## The three things to do first

Before anything else — before you try any workflow, before you look at any AI tool — do these three things.

### 1. Decide which AI tool is approved for official use

Pick one tool. It does not have to be the best tool in the world. It has to be one that your city has reviewed, that staff are allowed to use, and that everyone knows is the approved option.

The most common starting point for small cities is either Microsoft Copilot (if your city already uses Microsoft 365) or a business or enterprise AI subscription purchased by the city. The key word is purchased by the city — not a personal free-tier account.

National League of Cities research found that 68 percent of city employees use personal free-tier AI accounts for government work, and 57 percent input sensitive information into those accounts. Personal accounts are not covered by your city's data agreements. You have no control over how that data is used. That is the governance failure hiding in plain sight, and it is easy to fix: buy a city account and tell staff to use it instead.

If you cannot afford a paid account right now, it is better to permit limited use with clear rules than to prohibit AI entirely and watch staff use it anyway on personal accounts.

### 2. Tell staff what they can and cannot put into it

Before any AI tool is used for government work, staff need to know what is off-limits. This does not require a long policy. It requires a clear list.

Do not put these into any AI tool:
- Employee names, salaries, disciplinary records, or personnel file contents
- Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, or other personally identifiable information about residents
- Information protected by attorney-client privilege (anything your city attorney told you in confidence)
- Active law enforcement investigation details
- Pre-decisional budget information that has not been publicly released
- Information about pending litigation or settlement negotiations

Post this list somewhere visible. Put it in the staff meeting notes. Say it out loud. The people most likely to accidentally put sensitive information into an AI tool are not malicious — they are just busy and have not been told not to.

### 3. Establish that AI output is always reviewed by a human before it becomes official

This is the single most important rule. AI makes mistakes. It sometimes produces text that sounds authoritative but is wrong. It can misstate legal standards, get local facts wrong, and occasionally generate information that is simply fabricated.

The rule is simple: nothing produced by AI becomes an official city document, a public communication, a staff report, or a constituent response without a human being reading it first and deciding it is correct.

This does not mean AI is useless. It means AI is a first draft, not a final draft. A good first draft saves enormous time. A first draft that skips human review creates liability.

## The one policy you need to write

Every city that uses AI for government work should have a written acceptable use policy. It does not need to be long. A single page covers the essentials.

Here is a minimal but complete sample policy you can adapt. Fill in the bracketed fields with your city's specifics.

---

**[CITY NAME] Artificial Intelligence Acceptable Use Policy**

**Effective date:** [DATE]
**Adopted by:** [CITY COUNCIL / CITY MANAGER]
**Review date:** [DATE, recommend annual]

**Purpose.** This policy governs the use of artificial intelligence tools by city employees and officials in the course of their official duties.

**Approved tools.** The following AI tool(s) are approved for official city use: [LIST TOOL(S) BY NAME]. Use of other AI tools for official city business requires written approval from [CITY MANAGER / IT DIRECTOR / DEPARTMENT HEAD].

**Prohibited inputs.** Employees shall not enter the following categories of information into any AI tool:
- Personally identifiable information about residents or employees (Social Security numbers, financial account numbers, driver's license numbers, dates of birth combined with name and address)
- Personnel records, disciplinary files, or compensation data for individual employees
- Communications or documents protected by attorney-client privilege
- Active law enforcement investigation details or pre-arrest information
- Pre-decisional budget information that has not been publicly released
- Information about pending or threatened litigation or settlement negotiations

**Prohibition on personal accounts.** Employees shall not use personal AI accounts (free-tier or paid) for official city work. Official AI work shall be conducted only through city-approved accounts.

**Human review required.** No AI-generated content shall be released publicly, submitted as an official city document, sent as an official communication to a resident, or incorporated into any ordinance, resolution, staff report, or contract without review and approval by a city employee with appropriate authority for that document type. The reviewing employee is responsible for the accuracy of the final document.

**Records retention.** AI-generated documents and the prompts used to create them may be public records under state law. Employees shall retain AI-generated documents that informed official action under the same retention schedule that applies to that document type. Employees shall be prepared to retrieve AI conversation logs or prompts upon request if those materials relate to official city business. Questions about records retention obligations should be directed to the city clerk.

**Violations.** Use of AI tools in violation of this policy may result in disciplinary action consistent with city personnel policies.

**Questions.** Direct policy questions to [CITY MANAGER / DESIGNATED CONTACT].

---

This policy covers the core obligations without requiring legal expertise to draft. Have your city attorney review it before adoption, but this text gives them something to react to rather than drafting from scratch.

## What to reach for first

These five workflows deliver high value with low risk for a small city. They do not require specialized knowledge to use, and the consequences of an AI error are manageable because a human is always reviewing the output before it becomes official.

**Constituent response drafts.** A resident writes in about a pothole, a zoning question, or a noise complaint. AI can produce a draft response in seconds that covers the key facts, maintains a respectful tone, and points to the right next step. A staff member reads it, adjusts it, and sends it. Time savings: significant. Risk level: low, because a human is signing before it goes out.

**Meeting minutes.** AI can produce a first draft of meeting minutes from a recording transcript or a set of notes. The clerk still owns the official record and must review the draft against the recording before it is finalized. But generating the first draft is the hardest part, and AI handles it well. Motion language must be verified verbatim — that is the one place where the clerk cannot rely on a draft without checking.

**Staff report drafting.** AI is effective at producing the structure and boilerplate sections of a staff report — background, existing policy, options, fiscal impact framework. The staff member fills in the local facts, the city attorney reviews for legal accuracy, and the finance officer confirms fiscal figures. AI handles the scaffolding; your people provide the judgment.

**Grant opportunity screening.** A new federal or state grant program comes out. AI can read the program description and quickly tell you whether your city is likely eligible, what the application requires, and what the deadline is. This is a research and screening task — the kind of work that used to take hours and can now take minutes. The city still decides whether to apply, and a grant professional or city manager confirms the eligibility analysis.

**Public notice drafting.** AI can produce a draft public notice — for a public hearing, a road closure, a utility rate change — that is clear, readable, and covers the required elements. The city clerk verifies that it meets state statutory notice requirements (content, length, publication deadlines) before it goes out. AI drafts; the clerk certifies.

These five uses have something in common: they produce a draft that a human reviews before it matters. Start here before exploring higher-stakes applications.

## What not to do

**Do not launch a public-facing chatbot without legal review.** The most visible AI failure in municipal government so far is New York City's MyCity chatbot, launched in 2024. The system told businesses they could violate city law and gave confidently wrong information about tenant rights and employment regulations. The city had to publicly acknowledge the errors. The reputational and legal exposure from a public-facing chatbot that gives wrong legal information is substantial. Small cities that cannot afford a legal team to vet every possible response should not deploy public-facing chatbots. A well-maintained FAQ page is safer and often more useful.

**Do not use AI for disciplinary or enforcement decisions without documented human review.** If AI is used in any step of a process that affects someone's rights — a code enforcement citation, an employment termination, a benefits denial — and that decision is later challenged, the city will need to explain exactly what role AI played and who reviewed the outcome. AI decisions that are not documentably reviewed by a human create due process exposure. This is especially true for enforcement targeting (which addresses get cited), hiring and discipline, and benefits eligibility. Use AI to help draft the documentation, not to make the call.

**Do not use personal AI accounts for government work.** This point bears repeating because it is so common. When a staff member uses their personal AI chatbot account to draft a city document, that content is submitted under the employee's personal account with the employee's personal data agreement — not the city's. The city has no control over data retention, no ability to respond to a public records request for that conversation, and no insurance coverage for a data breach that runs through a personal account. The fix is inexpensive. Do it.

**Do not treat AI output as legal advice.** AI tools can describe what a law generally says. They cannot provide the legal analysis your city attorney would provide — grounded in your state's specific statutes, case law, local code, and the specific facts of your situation. Using AI output to make a legal decision without city attorney review is not a shortcut; it is a liability.

## When to get help

Some AI use cases require expert guidance before you proceed. Here is who to call.

**Call your state municipal league** before adopting an AI policy if your state has issued guidance on municipal AI use, before deploying AI in any function that touches records management or public records law, and when you want to know what comparable cities in your state are doing. Most state leagues have started tracking AI policy and can connect you with peers who have already worked through these questions.

**Call your city attorney** before deploying any AI tool that affects individual rights (enforcement, discipline, benefits), before responding to a public records request that involves AI-generated documents or prompts, before adopting a formal AI policy or acceptable use ordinance, and any time you are unsure whether an AI output is legally accurate enough to act on.

**Call your insurance carrier** before deploying a public-facing AI chatbot, before using AI in any process where an error could result in physical harm or significant financial harm to a resident, and when evaluating whether your existing liability coverage covers AI-related errors and omissions. Many municipal insurance pools have started issuing AI guidance — ask if yours has.

These calls are not admissions that you are doing something wrong. They are the same due diligence you would apply to any new operational practice. A short conversation before deployment is far cheaper than a legal defense or a public correction after the fact.
