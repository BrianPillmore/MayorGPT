## III. Walking Quorum Analysis

### The Doctrine
The walking quorum doctrine holds that serial communications among a majority of a governmental body, conducted outside a posted public meeting and involving deliberation on public business, can constitute a violation of open meetings law even if no single communication involves a quorum of the body. The doctrine prevents circumvention of the public meeting requirement by having members discuss public business one-at-a-time rather than in a single gathering.

### Elements Typically Required
Courts and attorney general opinions in most walking quorum jurisdictions have required a showing of:
1. **Serial contacts:** The member contacted others individually, not simultaneously in a group setting.
2. **Quorum-touching:** The total number of members contacted (plus the contacting member) equals or exceeds a quorum.
3. **Public business:** The subject matter was a pending matter subject to the governing body's authority.
4. **Deliberation:** The contacts involved discussion, exchange of views, or an attempt to commit votes — not merely informational sharing.

### Applying the Elements to the Alleged Facts

| Element | Alleged Facts | Risk Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Serial contacts | 4 individual contacts (2 calls, 2 emails) over 3 days | Confirmed pattern |
| Quorum-touching | Vasquez + 4 contacts = 5 of 7; quorum is 4; total exceeded quorum | High risk — mathematical threshold met |
| Public business | Subject was Contract CA-2026-041, a pending agenda item | High risk — clearly public business |
| Deliberation | Subject lines reference "thoughts on" and "following up on" the contract — suggests discussion, not mere notice | Medium-high risk — depends on content |

**Key unknown:** The actual content of the calls and emails. If the communications were purely informational (e.g., Vasquez sharing the staff report summary) rather than deliberative (Vasquez seeking commitments or expressing views on how members should vote), the deliberation element may not be met. ## IV. Criminal Exposure — Section 551.143

Section 551.143 creates a criminal offense for "knowingly conspiring to circumvent" the Act. This is a higher bar than a civil violation.

**Risk assessment:** The criminal exposure risk is lower than the civil/administrative risk, but cannot be dismissed without reviewing the communications. The word "knowingly" requires evidence of intent to circumvent the Act, not merely inadvertent contact. Whether a series of individual contacts about a pending vote demonstrates "knowing conspiracy" is a fact-intensive inquiry.

**Practical note:** Even a finding of no criminal exposure does not resolve the civil/transparency issue. The city's obligation to protect public trust is not limited to criminal liability.

> **Annotation — Separating criminal from civil risk:** Many officials panic when "criminal" is mentioned in an open meetings context. The AI correctly contextualizes this: the criminal bar (knowing conspiracy) is higher than the civil/administrative bar (holding a meeting without proper notice). This distinction matters for how the city attorney frames the risk to the council.
