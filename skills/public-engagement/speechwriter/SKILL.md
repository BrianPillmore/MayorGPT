# Speechwriter

**Purpose:** Write remarks that sound like the speaker, serve a clear purpose, and keep official promises and facts accurate.

## Required inputs
- Speaker, role, and how they naturally talk (past speeches or samples)
- Event, audience, length (words ≈ minutes x 130), setting, other speakers, and media presence
- The one message to land and the one action to ask for
- Verified facts, numbers, names, and approved stories; sensitivities and what must not be said

## Workflow
1. **Frame:** audience, purpose (inform, honor, persuade, reassure), and "one sentence the audience should remember."
2. **Structure:** opening that connects to place or people (not "Good evening, thank you for having me" alone); one to three points, each with a concrete example or number; a story rooted in a real resident or project with permission; a close that names the next step.
3. **Fact check** every claim against sources; label estimates; say "approximately." Do not announce decisions that the council has not made or details under embargo.
4. **Voice:** short sentences, active verbs, local references, plain words. Match the speaker's humor and formality level. Avoid cliches and borrowed lines; do not copy others' text without attribution.
5. **Legal and ethical checks:** no campaign or candidate advocacy at official events (verify rules on ballot measures and public funds); no comments on pending litigation, personnel, quasi-judicial matters (see `ethics-conflict-check`); respect confidential closed-session material. Include acknowledgments in correct protocol order (verify local protocol) and pronounce names right.
6. **Delivery aids:** a speaking copy with breaks and emphasis, time marks, pronunciation guide, and a 90-second cut. Accessible slides or handouts if used.
7. **Anticipate Q&A or hecklers**: two or three lines for tough questions and a plan for interruptions.
8. **Record:** speeches may be public records and widely quoted; keep the final text.

## Output structure
- Speech text with timings
- Source/fact table for each claim
- 90-second version and 3 quotable lines
- Do-not-say list and Q&A prep

## Example opening
> "Eleven years ago, this lot was a place people crossed the street to avoid. Tonight, it is a library with a line out the door."

## Red flags / escalate to the city attorney
- Speech touches litigation, labor negotiations, land-use cases before the body, or elections
- Promises of funding or timelines not yet approved
- Quotes or stories involving identifiable residents without permission

## Common mistakes
- Too many points and statistics
- Reading dense policy language aloud
- Overpromising for applause

*Not legal advice.*
