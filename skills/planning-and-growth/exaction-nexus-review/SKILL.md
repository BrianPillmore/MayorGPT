## Purpose
Reduce takings and unconstitutional-conditions risk by documenting why each exaction is justified. This skill structures the analysis; it is not a legal opinion, and the city attorney decides whether a condition is lawful.

## Required inputs
- The proposed exaction (land dedication, easement, street improvement, fee, affordable housing requirement) and its source: negotiated, ordinance-based, or discretionary.
- Project description, trip generation, demand on utilities, parks, and public safety.
- Fee study or impact analysis, capital plan, and ordinance text.
- Whether the exaction is legislative (applies to a class by formula) or adjudicative (imposed on one project).
- Any prior communication or agreement with the applicant.

## Workflow
1. Classify the exaction. Land-use dedications and money conditions are both reviewed in many courts; recent U.S. Supreme Court decisions (Nollan, Dolan, Koontz, Sheetz) extend scrutiny to monetary demands and to legislatively imposed fees in at least some contexts. The attorney should confirm current law and the state rule.
2. Essential nexus: identify the specific public impact the project creates and show the condition addresses that impact (for example, new trips and the intersection improvement). Record the evidence.
3. Rough proportionality: make an individualized determination that the condition's nature and extent are roughly proportional to the project's impact. Quantify both sides (cost of required improvement and the project's share of the demand). Avoid cost allocation that makes the developer fix an existing deficiency.
4. Existing deficiency check: if the condition remedies a pre-existing problem, separate that portion and fund it publicly or through a program.
5. Credits and reimbursement: oversizing beyond project need should be reimbursed or credited through a defined mechanism (latecomer agreement, fee credit). Document the formula.
6. Fee mechanics: use a current study with a service area, level-of-service standard (for example square feet of park per capita, trip capacity), and cost per unit; fees must be spent on the identified capital within a time limit under many state statutes. Check refund and annual accounting requirements.
7. Alternatives: offer options (dedication or fee in lieu, phased improvement) so the applicant can choose the least burdensome.
8. Record: document findings in the staff report and resolution; ensure the applicant had notice and opportunity to object, and preserve the record for any challenge.

## Output structure
| Exaction | Project impact (quantified) | Nexus evidence | Proportionality calc | Existing deficiency share | Credit/reimbursement | Risk (L/M/H) |
Add: recommended modification (reduce, restructure, fund publicly) and attorney questions.

## Red flags / escalate
- Exaction unrelated to the project's impact (for example, a trail dedication for an unrelated housing project).
- Negotiated "voluntary" contribution that is really a condition of approval.
- Fee based on an outdated study or averaged across unlike uses.
- Fee revenue used for operations or for unrelated capital.
- Applicant objection on the record: city attorney immediately.

## Common mistakes
- Citing a general plan goal instead of a project impact.
- No individualized finding for a discretionary condition.
- Using proportionality language without numbers.
- Forgetting the applicant's alternatives and credits.

## State-law variance
State impact-fee enabling statutes, time limits, refunds, and exaction standards vary widely and can be stricter than federal law.
