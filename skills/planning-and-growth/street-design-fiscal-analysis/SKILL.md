## Purpose
Add a fiscal-productivity view to corridor decisions that are otherwise judged only on vehicle throughput and level of service (LOS). Safety, emergency access, freight, transit, and ADA remain governing constraints; this analysis informs trade-offs, it does not override engineering judgment or crash data.

## Required inputs
- Corridor limits, current cross-section, posted and observed speeds, crash history (by severity and mode), traffic volumes, transit and freight use.
- Proposed project scope, cost, funding source (federal/state funds carry design standards), and engineering justification.
- Adjacent parcels: land uses, assessed value, acreage, vacancy, ground-floor activity, parking supply.
- Comparable corridors in the city and the adopted street design standards or complete streets policy.
- Lifecycle maintenance cost per lane-mile or per square yard, and the pavement/underground condition.

## Workflow
1. Classify the corridor by function: street (low speed, direct access, active frontage), road (connector, limited access), or stroad (high-speed design with frequent driveways and conflict points). State evidence: speeds, access points per mile, frontage uses, pedestrian crossings, crash pattern.
2. Classify the direction of the proposed change: toward street, toward road, or toward stroad (added lanes/turn lanes, wider lanes, higher design speed, fewer pedestrian accommodations). If the change is described as safety, test whether the design reduces serious-crash risk with evidence (speed reduction, conflict reduction).
3. Value baseline: assessed value per adjacent acre versus the city average and comparable corridors by type, using the same assessor source. Include the public cost per lane-mile as a ratio to adjacent taxable value.
4. Effect estimate: direction and order of magnitude only, with the evidence base (local before/after cases, published studies) named; avoid precise predictions. Note that causality between street design and land value is context dependent.
5. Lifecycle: add the added pavement and signal maintenance obligation for the proposal and compare to expected tax gain.
6. Conflict statement: identify where engineering metrics (LOS, volume-to-capacity) and fiscal productivity point in different directions, and quantify where possible. Show both to decision-makers.
7. Options: lower-cost alternatives (lane reconfiguration, access management, turn restrictions, signal timing, crossings, speed management) and temporary pilots using paint, planters, and quick-build materials to test effects before permanent construction.
8. Funding and standards: federal and state funding often require engineering standards and review (design speed, clear zones); confirm what flexibility exists before promising alternatives.

## Output structure
| Segment | Current type | Proposed direction | Adjacent value/acre | Crash profile | Capital cost | Added O&M/yr | Notes |
Add: conflict statement, pilot option with cost versus permanent, and recommendation with decision points.

## Red flags / escalate
- Safety claims unsupported by crash data or speed management.
- Widening that removes parking, trees, or storefront access on an active commercial street.
- Project dependent on a funding source that dictates design standards.
- State route or county road with another owner's approval needed.
- Emergency-response access, ADA, or transit conflicts: involve the public works director, fire chief, and a licensed traffic engineer.

## Common mistakes
- Using LOS as the sole measure.
- Claiming fiscal benefit without local comparables.
- Ignoring maintenance cost of added lanes.
- Treating a pilot as a final design.

## State-law variance
Design standard adoption, speed-limit setting authority, and DOT approvals vary by state.
