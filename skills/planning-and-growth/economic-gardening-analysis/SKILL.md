## Purpose
Compare two uses of public economic-development dollars: structured support for established local growth companies (economic gardening) versus incentives to recruit an outside employer. The comparison is fiscal, uses local data, and treats claims about outcomes as assumptions to test, not as findings.

## Required inputs
- Sector or project in question, council goals, and any pending recruitment proposal with its incentive terms.
- Local business data: licenses, employer counts, sales tax reports (aggregated), chamber/SBDC/lender intelligence.
- Assessed values and building footprints for candidate firms; infrastructure condition around them.
- Program cost options (staff, consultants, market data, peer networks, capital connections).

## Workflow
1. Define candidates: firms already operating in the city, past startup stage (a revenue band such as roughly $500,000 to $10 million is a common screen; adjust locally), with evidence of growth (revenue, hiring, expansion inquiries), in buildings already served by infrastructure. Use aggregated or consented data; do not publish confidential business information.
2. Interview a sample to test real constraints: financing, workforce, space, permitting, market access. Support only helps if it addresses those constraints.
3. Scenario: build conservative, base, and strong growth cases over three to five years (a base of about 30 percent revenue growth is a planning assumption, not a prediction). Convert to incremental assessed value, sales tax, jobs, and spending effects with stated multipliers.
4. Program cost: itemize and compare per job and per dollar of new taxable value. State what portion of growth is attributable to the program (additionality) and haircut accordingly.
5. Recruitment comparison: for the recruitment proposal, document incentive cost over the full term, public infrastructure required, assessed value per acre, annual net fiscal contribution after the incentive period, and payback.
6. Risk comparison: concentration and exit risk for recruitment (infrastructure remains, revenue leaves; precedent for future demands) versus execution risk for gardening (diffuse, harder to attribute, requires sustained staff capacity).
7. Side-by-side table across total public cost, new infrastructure obligation, annual net contribution, payback, jobs, exit risk, and time to first return.
8. Recommend an approach, a pilot size, evaluation metrics, and a stop rule.

## Output structure
| Dimension | Recruitment | Economic gardening |
Rows: total public cost; infrastructure obligation; annual net fiscal contribution; payback; jobs and cost per job (including infrastructure); exit risk; time to first return; attribution confidence.
Add: candidate list (anonymized if needed), assumptions, and pilot plan.

## Red flags / escalate
- But-for case for recruitment cannot be documented.
- Infrastructure for the recruited site exceeds multiple years of program cost.
- Strong local firms exist without any structured support.
- Candidate-firm data confidentiality or conflict-of-interest issues (council members tied to firms).
- Program funds being used as de facto grants to individual firms: legal review of public-purpose/gift-of-public-funds restrictions.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming program success without additionality.
- Excluding infrastructure from cost per job.
- Choosing candidates by familiarity rather than data.
- Declaring the approaches mutually exclusive.

## State-law variance
Public-purpose doctrine, confidentiality of business data, and permitted economic-development spending vary by state.
