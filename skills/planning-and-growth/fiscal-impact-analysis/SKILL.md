## Purpose
Show council the net, lifecycle fiscal effect of a development, not just its headline tax revenue. A project that produces tax revenue but requires public infrastructure and ongoing service costs the revenue never covers adds to the city's long-term liability.

## Required inputs
- Project program: land uses, units or square feet, acreage, phasing, and absorption schedule.
- Assessor data for comparable parcels and the assessor's likely valuation method; current tax rates from every taxing jurisdiction and the city's share.
- City cost data: per-capita, per-unit, per-call, or per-lane-mile costs by department; fire/EMS and police workload; road and utility inventories and unit replacement costs.
- Public improvements required (roads, water, sewer, drainage, signals, parks) and who pays.
- Impact fees, exactions, and any incentives.

## Workflow
1. Annual revenue at stabilization: property tax (city share only), sales/lodging/utility taxes if applicable, franchise and fee revenue, and shared revenue. Use comparable assessed values, not the developer's valuation. Remove double counting when the project replaces an existing use.
2. Annual cost of services: use a marginal or case-study approach. Average cost overstates for infill and understates for edge growth; state which method and why. Include public works, public safety, parks, library, administration, and city-operated utilities (net of utility revenue).
3. Capital: total public capital the project requires or triggers, split by funded by developer/exactions, impact fees, and city. Add future replacement cost: annual set-aside is replacement value divided by useful life.
4. Net annual fiscal contribution = annual revenue minus annual service cost minus annual lifecycle capital set-aside for public assets added.
5. Payback = city-funded capital divided by annual net contribution; if net is negative, state there is no payback and quantify the perpetual annual deficit.
6. Value per acre: assessed value per acre for the subject versus city land-use categories using the same assessor source, and versus the existing use. Where local data are lacking, widely cited illustrative ranges (low for auto-oriented single-use, higher for mixed-use) may be cited only as labeled, non-local context. A redevelopment lowering value per acre on land already served is a fiscal regression.
7. Growth-dependency test: if the project needs new infrastructure extensions, has low value per acre, and payback exceeds 30 years or net is negative, flag it as fiscally unsustainable: early fees and permits mask long-term obligations. (30 years is a screening convention, not a legal standard.)
8. Sensitivity: valuation 25 percent lower, slower absorption, higher cost per call, and incentive terms. Show the break-even assessed value.
9. Distinguish one-time (permit fees, construction sales tax) from recurring revenue.

## Output structure
| Metric | Year 1 | Year 5 | Stabilized | Notes |
Rows: recurring revenue, one-time revenue, recurring service cost, lifecycle capital set-aside, net annual contribution; then capital investment, payback, value per acre vs. comparables, and sensitivity table.

## Red flags / escalate
- Approval based on headline tax revenue without cost-of-service analysis.
- City-funded infrastructure larger than 10 years of net contribution.
- Negative net contribution with an incentive on top.
- School or special-district costs ignored when the city funds them.
- Assumptions the assessor or county will not confirm.
- Public-notice or findings requirements for an incentive or development agreement.

## Common mistakes
- Using developer valuations.
- Ignoring maintenance and replacement.
- Applying average costs to a big edge project.
- Counting construction-period revenue as recurring.

## State-law variance
Assessment methods, levy limits, and revenue sharing differ by state; confirm with the assessor and finance director.
