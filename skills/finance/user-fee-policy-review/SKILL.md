## Purpose
Test whether a general-fund fee or charge (permits, inspections, recreation, EMS transport, development review, rental licenses) recovers an intended share of full cost, is legally defensible as a fee rather than a tax, and is set deliberately rather than by habit. For enterprise utility rates, use enterprise-fund-rate-study.

## Required inputs
- Current fee schedule, volumes by fee for three years, and revenue by fee.
- Direct costs (labor with benefits, supplies, contracts) and a method for indirect and overhead costs (central service cost allocation plan).
- Council policy on cost recovery (full, partial, subsidized, or market).
- Peer fees, but with notes on service level differences.
- Legal authority to charge, and any state-imposed caps.

## Workflow
1. Define the service unit and who benefits (private benefit vs. public benefit vs. mixed). Private-benefit services (building permits, plan review) typically support high cost recovery; broad public-benefit services (parks, public safety) are usually tax-supported, with deliberate exceptions.
2. Compute full cost: direct labor (productive hours, not paid hours), supplies, vehicle/IT, departmental overhead, central services allocation, and capital replacement where appropriate. Document the allocation method.
3. Compare to revenue: cost recovery percent = fee revenue divided by full cost. Compute by fee, not just in aggregate, to find cross-subsidies among fee types.
4. Legal test: fees must bear a reasonable relationship to cost of the service in most states; charges above cost can be recharacterized as taxes. Check for state limits, voter approval requirements for new taxes or fee increases, and rules that restrict use of fee revenue to the regulated service (many states require earmarking permit revenue).
5. Equity and policy: affordability for low-income users, fee waivers (and who funds the waiver), housing, small business, and nonprofit effects. Do not use fees to subsidize one group invisibly.
6. Peer context: use peers only after normalizing for service level, geography, and cost basis. Never set fees solely by matching neighbors.
7. Options: raise to target recovery, phase in, index annually to a stated inflation measure, restructure tiers, or maintain with explicit subsidy. Show revenue effect, volume elasticity assumptions, and impact on typical users.
8. Communication plan: public notice, comment timeline, effective date, and plain-language explanation of what the fee pays for.

## Output structure
| Fee | Volume | Full cost per unit | Current fee | Recovery % | Policy target | Proposed | Revenue effect | Legal note |

Add: cross-subsidy summary, phase-in schedule, waiver policy, and public notice checklist.

## Red flags / escalate
- Fee revenue exceeding cost of service or diverted to unrelated uses (tax-versus-fee risk).
- Fees set by ordinance but collected differently, or waivers granted ad hoc.
- Fee increases requiring election or supermajority.
- Impact fees or exactions: route to exaction-nexus-review and the city attorney.
- Public hearing or notice requirements not met.

## Common mistakes
- Using paid hours rather than productive hours.
- Omitting overhead or capital.
- Assuming volume stays constant after large increases.
- Ignoring the legal earmark for permit revenue.
- Matching peer fees without checking service levels.

## State-law variance
Fee authority, earmarking, indexing, and notice rules differ by state; confirm with the city attorney.
