## Purpose
Test whether proposed utility rates cover the full cost of an enterprise fund and are allocated fairly among customer classes. This skill reviews and explains a rate study; it does not replace a qualified rate consultant or the city attorney on rate-setting law.

## Required inputs
- Audited financials and the current-year budget for the fund; three to five years of customer counts, billed volumes, and revenue.
- Capital improvement plan for the utility, existing debt schedule and covenants, and the asset register with replacement values.
- Current rate ordinance, tier structure, connection and impact fees, and any general fund transfers or payments in lieu of taxes.
- Reserve policy (operating, capital, debt service) and customer class data (residential, commercial, industrial, wholesale).
- Any consultant study and its assumptions.

## Workflow
1. Revenue requirement (cash-needs or utility-basis, whichever the covenant or practice uses): operating and maintenance, debt service, cash-funded capital, reserve contributions, and transfers. Confirm every transfer to the general fund has legal basis and a documented cost rationale.
2. Test capital adequacy: compare annual capital funding to depreciation or replacement-need estimates. A fund that recovers only historical-cost depreciation will undersave for replacement; say so.
3. Project multi-year: inflation, growth in connections, volume decline from conservation, chemical/energy cost drivers, regulatory mandates, and debt issuance. Show rate path under base and stress scenarios.
4. Coverage: calculate net revenue available for debt service divided by annual debt service and compare to the rate covenant (often 1.20x or higher, per the bond documents; use the actual covenant).
5. Cost-of-service allocation: functionalize costs (supply, treatment, distribution, collection, customer service), allocate to classes by demand (peak, average) and customer-related cost drivers using an accepted methodology (for water, the AWWA M1 manual). Check no class is systematically subsidized beyond what law allows.
6. Rate design: fixed vs. volumetric split (revenue stability vs. conservation signal), tiering justification, low-income assistance (legal limits on subsidy and funding source), outside-city surcharges (must be supportable), and seasonal factors.
7. Legal test: most states require rates to be reasonable and proportional to service. Some states, notably California under Proposition 218, add notice and protest procedures; others require regulator approval. Confirm applicable process, notice period, and challenge window.
8. Affordability: compare average bill to median household income by neighborhood and show the bill impact for low, median, and high users. Describe options (phased increases, assistance program).

## Output structure
| Year | Revenue requirement | Revenue at current rates | Gap % | Required rate adjustment | Debt coverage (x) | Reserve days |
Add: class cost-of-service table, bill impact table, risk register, and ordinance/notice checklist.

## Red flags / escalate
- Coverage near or below the covenant, or reserves below policy.
- Transfers to the general fund exceeding documented cost, or using utility revenue for non-utility purposes.
- Deferred capital while rates are held flat ("rate freeze" with growing backlog).
- Unlawful cross-subsidy or rate class that cannot be defended.
- Required notice or protest procedure not on the calendar.
- Regulatory consent order or lead service line mandates.

## Common mistakes
- Setting rates from last year's budget instead of a multi-year requirement.
- Ignoring volume decline.
- Treating a fixed charge as a tax.
- Neglecting affordability analysis.
- Deferring many years of increases and then needing a shock.

## State-law variance
Rate-setting procedure, notice, protest rights, regulator review, and permitted transfers vary strongly by state. Verify before scheduling hearings.
