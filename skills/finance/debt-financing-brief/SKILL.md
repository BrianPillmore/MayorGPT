## Purpose
Give council and the manager a decision-ready comparison of ways to pay for a capital need (cash, grants, state revolving loans, general obligation, revenue bonds, lease-purchase, interfund loan) with debt capacity, affordability, and approval pathway stated plainly. This brief informs; it is not a recommendation from a registered municipal advisor and not legal opinion from bond counsel.

## Required inputs
- Project description, cost estimate and date, useful life, and the asset's revenue source (enterprise rates, property tax, special assessment).
- Existing debt schedule: outstanding principal, annual debt service by fund, call dates, covenants, ratings.
- Legal debt limit and how it is measured (percent of assessed value, per statute or charter), plus any debt policy ratios.
- Revenue pledged or available, historical coverage, and reserve balances.
- Timeline: construction start, grant deadlines, election dates.

## Workflow
1. Define the need and life: match the term to the asset's useful life, never beyond it. Do not finance operating costs or short-lived equipment on long terms.
2. List options: pay-go/reserves, grants, state or federal low-interest loans (for example state revolving funds, where eligible), GO bonds, revenue bonds, special assessment/improvement district debt, certificates of participation or lease-purchase, short-term notes, developer financing. Mark which are legally available in the state.
3. For each option, record: security/pledge, approval needed (council vote, supermajority, voter referendum, state approval), legal limit consumed, covenants (rate covenant, additional-bonds test, reserve fund), and typical timing.
4. Model affordability with placeholders clearly labeled as assumptions: illustrative rate, term, issuance costs. Show annual debt service, debt service as a share of the relevant fund's operating revenue, and for property-tax-backed debt the effect on a median home. Do not present a rate quote as fact; recommend a municipal advisor for pricing.
5. Test capacity: debt per capita and to assessed value, debt service share of expenditures, coverage ratio for revenue debt (net revenue to debt service, against the covenant), and effect on the rating outlook.
6. Stress the repayment source: revenue downturn, rate-increase political risk, construction overruns, and variable-rate or balloon exposure.
7. Identify reserve effects: capitalized interest, debt service reserve, and whether reserves fall below policy.
8. Lay out the approval path and calendar working back from the date funds are needed, including referendum deadlines, bond counsel and advisor engagement, disclosure documents, and post-issuance compliance (arbitrage, continuing disclosure).

## Output structure
| Option | Security | Approval path | Est. annual debt service* | Limit consumed | Key covenants | Main risk | Earliest closing |
(*illustrative; labeled assumptions)

Add: recommendation range (not a pricing quote), one-page plain-language summary for the public, and decision calendar.

## Red flags / escalate
- Debt limit or coverage covenant within a small margin after the issue.
- Lease structure used to avoid a required vote (bond counsel must review).
- Financing a recurring operating gap with debt.
- Pledge of general fund for a project that should be self-supporting.
- Any election or disclosure timing issue: involve bond counsel and the municipal advisor before council acts.
- Tax-exempt status concerns (private use, reimbursement resolution timing).

## Common mistakes
- Quoting rates or savings that are not market-based.
- Ignoring issuance costs and reserve requirements.
- Matching a 30-year term to a 15-year asset.
- Forgetting the O&M cost of the asset financed.
- Assuming revenue growth that has not occurred.

## State-law variance
Debt limits, voter approval thresholds, authorized instruments, and lease-purchase rules differ significantly by state. Cite statute only after counsel confirms.
